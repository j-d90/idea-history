**Many hands.** Cavaillon and Legout (2022) list precursors going back to Girolamo Fracastoro (1546), who thought diseases spread through "seminaria," or seeds. The theory's 19th-century foundations were laid by several people:

- **Semmelweis (1847)** showed that medical students' hands, after autopsies, carried infection to women giving birth.
- **Pasteur (from 1860–61)** took up the Academy of Sciences' challenge to settle the question of spontaneous generation. The decisive swan-neck flask experiments, which showed that the air carries germs, were conceived by his colleague Antoine Balard.
- **Davaine (1863)** found bacteria in the blood of animals with anthrax and showed that the disease spread through infected blood. Historians credit him with the first demonstration that a bacterium causes disease.
- **Lister (1865)** learned of Pasteur's theory and applied it to surgery, using phenol as an antiseptic.
- **Koch (1876)** published his study of the anthrax bacillus. Blevins and Bronze (2010) say this work "launched the field of medical bacteriology."

**Myth and reality.** Cavaillon and Legout write explicitly to separate Pasteur's real achievements from "the legend built by his hagiographers," in part one Pasteur cultivated himself. Crediting the theory to one or two famous names misrepresents how it was built.
