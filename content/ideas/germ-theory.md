**Many hands.** Precursors of germ theory go back at least to Girolamo Fracastoro (1546), who thought diseases spread through "seminaria," or seeds [@cavaillon-2022]. The theory's 19th-century foundations were laid by several people:

- **Semmelweis (1847)** showed that medical students' hands, after autopsies, carried infection to women giving birth [@cavaillon-2022].
- **Pasteur (from 1860–61)** took up the Academy of Sciences' challenge to settle the question of spontaneous generation. The decisive swan-neck flask experiments, which showed that the air carries germs, were conceived by his colleague Antoine Balard [@cavaillon-2022].
- **Davaine (1863)** found bacteria in the blood of animals with anthrax and showed that the disease spread through infected blood. Historians credit him with the first demonstration that a bacterium causes disease [@cavaillon-2022].
- **Lister (1865)** learned of Pasteur's theory and applied it to surgery, using phenol as an antiseptic [@cavaillon-2022].
- **Koch (1876)** published his study of the anthrax bacillus [@koch-1876]. This work "launched the field of medical bacteriology" [@blevins-2010].

**Myth and reality.** Cavaillon and Legout write explicitly to separate Pasteur's real achievements from "the legend built by his hagiographers," in part a legend he cultivated himself [@cavaillon-2022]. Crediting the theory to one or two famous names misrepresents how it was built.
