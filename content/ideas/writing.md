**Precursors.** Before writing, Near Eastern administrators kept accounts with small clay tokens, some sealed inside clay envelopes ("bullae"). Englund (2004, p. 100) calls the tokens' role as forerunners of the earliest tablets, first argued by Denise Schmandt-Besserat, "core knowledge" among cuneiformists. Many of the numerical signs on early tablets match the tokens in form and function.

**Dating.** Woods (2010, pp. 34–35) divides the earliest tablets into two script phases: Uruk IV (c. 3200 BCE) and Uruk III (c. 3100 BCE). The German Archaeological Institute excavated about 5,000 proto-cuneiform tablets at Uruk between 1928 and 1976. Englund counts the whole proto-cuneiform corpus at over 6,000 tablets and fragments.

**Purpose.** Nearly all the earliest texts are administrative. Literature, religious texts, historical documents and letters appear only from the mid-third millennium BCE, some 700 years after the first written evidence (Woods 2010, p. 34).

**Independent inventions.** Woods (2010, p. 15) counts four "pristine" writing systems, invented with no knowledge of writing elsewhere: Mesopotamia and Egypt at the end of the fourth millennium BCE, China at the end of the second millennium BCE, and Mesoamerica by the middle of the first millennium BCE.
