**The first city.** In the Late Uruk period (c. 3350–3100 BCE), Uruk grew to 20,000–50,000 people and became "arguably, the world's first true city." The new administration needed to track production, goods and labour, and writing was invented in that setting [@woods-2010-mesopotamia, p. 34].

**Precursors.** Before writing, Near Eastern administrators kept accounts with small clay tokens, some sealed inside clay envelopes ("bullae"). That the tokens were forerunners of the earliest tablets, first argued by Denise Schmandt-Besserat, is "core knowledge" among cuneiformists. Many of the numerical signs on early tablets match the tokens in form and function [@englund-2004, p. 100].

**Dating.** The earliest tablets fall into two script phases: Uruk IV (c. 3200 BCE) and Uruk III (c. 3100 BCE). The German Archaeological Institute excavated about 5,000 proto-cuneiform tablets at Uruk between 1928 and 1976 [@woods-2010-mesopotamia, p. 35]. The whole proto-cuneiform corpus runs to over 6,000 tablets and fragments [@englund-2004, p. 100].

**Purpose.** Nearly all the earliest texts are administrative. Literature, religious texts, historical documents and letters appear only from the mid-third millennium BCE, some 700 years after the first written evidence [@woods-2010-mesopotamia, p. 34].

**Independent inventions.** There are four "pristine" writing systems, invented with no knowledge of writing elsewhere: Mesopotamia and Egypt at the end of the fourth millennium BCE, China at the end of the second millennium BCE, and Mesoamerica by the middle of the first millennium BCE [@woods-2010-intro, p. 15].
