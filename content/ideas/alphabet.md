**Invention.** The earliest alphabetic inscriptions come from Serabit el-Khadem, an Egyptian mining site in Sinai, and from Wadi el-Hol near Thebes [@lam-2010, p. 189]. The two Wadi el-Hol inscriptions date to roughly 1900–1800 BCE [@herrmann-2023]. "The alphabet was invented among Semitic speakers in the Egyptian realm, inspired iconographically by hieroglyphic writing but not bound by its modes of expression" [@lam-2010, p. 190]. Workers at the Sinai turquoise mines, or Canaanites living in Egypt, are the likeliest inventors [@herrmann-2023].

**How it worked.** Each sign took its sound from the first consonant of the West Semitic word for the thing pictured (the *acrophonic principle*): a hand, *kaph*, stood for /k/ [@lam-2010, pp. 189–190]. Alphabets need tens of signs where logographic and syllabic systems need hundreds. Most of the world's common languages, Chinese and Japanese aside, are written in scripts descended from this linear West Semitic alphabet [@lam-2010, p. 189].

**Development.** Proto-Canaanite inscriptions in the Levant run from the 17th century BCE, and most date from the 13th century or later. Around 1000 BCE the script stabilized as a 22-letter alphabet written right to left, matching the sounds of Phoenician. Hebrew and Aramaic speakers adopted it, and the oldest Greek inscriptions appear in the 8th century BCE [@lam-2010, pp. 191–193].

**A caution about literacy.** It is tempting to say the alphabet put literacy within everyone's reach. Lam argues against this: although it probably made scribal training easier, "no immediate correlation can be made between alphabetic writing and broad literacy" [@lam-2010, p. 189].
