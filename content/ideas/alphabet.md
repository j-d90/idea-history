**Invention.** Lam (2010, p. 190) concludes that "the alphabet was invented among Semitic speakers in the Egyptian realm, inspired iconographically by hieroglyphic writing but not bound by its modes of expression." Signs took their sound from the first consonant of the West Semitic word for the thing pictured (the *acrophonic principle*): a hand, *kaph*, stood for /k/. Herrmann (2023) dates the two Wadi el-Hol inscriptions to roughly 1900–1800 BCE. Workers at Egypt's Sinai turquoise mines, or Canaanites living in Egypt, are the likeliest inventors.

**Development.** Proto-Canaanite inscriptions in the Levant run from the 17th century BCE, and most date from the 13th century or later. Around 1000 BCE the script stabilized as a 22-letter alphabet written right to left, matching the sounds of Phoenician. Hebrew and Aramaic speakers adopted it, and the oldest Greek inscriptions appear in the 8th century BCE (Lam 2010, pp. 191–193).

**A caution about literacy.** It is tempting to say the alphabet put literacy within everyone's reach. Lam (2010, p. 189) argues against this: although it probably made scribal training easier, "no immediate correlation can be made between alphabetic writing and broad literacy."
